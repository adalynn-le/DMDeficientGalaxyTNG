\subsection{IllustrisTNG 300-1}
\label{IllustrisTNG 300-1}

The IllustrisTNG 300-1 (referred to as TNG 300-1)magnetohydrodynamical simulation is the largest-volume realization in the TNG series, modeling baryon-inclusive galaxy formation on a simulation scale of \(300 \textup{ Mpc}\) \cite{nelson2021illustristngsimulationspublicdata,Pillepich_2017,Weinberger_2016}. The simulation employs Planck 2015 results ($\Omega_{\Lambda, 0} = 0.6911, \Omega_{m,0}=0.3089, \Omega_{b,0}=0.0486, \sigma_8=0.8159, n_s=0.9667 $ and $h=0.6774$) and Newtonian self-gravity \cite{Planck2016}. The resolutions are $m_\textup{baryon}=7.6 \times 10^6$ and $m_\textup{DM} = 4.0 \times 10^7$. The softening length for dark matter is $\epsilon_{\textup{DM}\star}=2.0 \rightarrow 1.0$ TNG 300-1 spans 100 snapshots (from redshift $z=127$ to $z=0.0$). TNG 300-1 employs friend-of-friend (FoF)  algorithim to identify halos \cite{1985ApJ...292..371D}, SUBFIND identify subhaloes \cite{2001MNRAS.328..726S}, and SUBLINK to identify and construct merger trees \cite{Rodriguez_Gomez_2016, 2015MNRAS.449...49R}. The TNG catalog contains $14,485,709$ subhalos.
\subsection{Measurements and Models}
\label{Measurements and Models}
We employ the fraction of dark matter mass ($f_\textup{DM}$) as a measure of dark matter deficiency. This value is calculated using the formula in Equation \eqref{fDM Calculation}, where $M_\textup{DM}<2R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ is the total mass of the dark matter particles enclosed within twice the subhalo half-mass radius, and $M<2R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ is the total particle mass enclosed within twice the subhalo half-mass radius, within a spherical aperture of radius $2R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ centered on the subhalo \cite{Jing_2019}. Analysis is carried out at different snapshot values which are translated to redshift via TNG 300-1's data release. We employ the use and analysis of other measures ($M_\star, \textup{Position}$, etc.) using what is reported by TNG 300-1's catalog. A DMDG is classified as one that meets the criteria established in \ref{Galaxy Selection Pipeline} and has $f_\textup{DM} < 0.5$. This is consistent with the analysis of Jing et al. \cite{Jing_2019} and employed throughout all trials. We also separate DMDGs between "extreme" and non-extreme DMDGs to account for the potential effects of tidal dwarf galaxies formed in tidal tails of interacting galaxies or poorly identified subhaloes \cite{2018MNRAS.474..580P}. Our criteria for non-extreme DMDGs is that $f_\textup{DM}>0.05$.  
\begin{equation}
f_\textup{DM} = \frac{M_\textup{DM} < 2R_{\textup{half}, \star}}{M_\textup{total} < 2R_{\textup{half}, \star}}
\label{fDM Calculation}
\end{equation}
\subsection{Galaxy Selection Pipeline}
\label{Galaxy Selection Pipeline}
We divide our study into a reproduction and renewed analysis of Jing et al. \cite{Jing_2019} using modern methods and techniques and the scale of TNG 300-1 (Section \ref{Literature Comparisson and Extended History Analysis}), and a broader-scale analysis featuring different criteria for DMDGs and included galaxies (Section \ref{Broad Analysis of DMDG Subhaloes}). We feature different definitions and selection pipelines for these  sections. We require a stellar resolution of ($N_\star \geq 500$), and TNG catalog classification as a subhalo ($\textup{SubhaloFlag = 1}$) to be considered a resolved galaxy. This criteria reduces our sample to $154,638$ resolved galaxies. 

For Section \ref{Literature Comparisson and Extended History Analysis}, we employ the selection pipeline used in Jing et al. (stellar mass $10^9 < M_\star < 10^{10} M_\odot$, host halo mass $M_{200} > 10^{13} M_\odot$, and a classification as a satellite galaxy by the TNG 300-1 catalog) \cite{Jing_2019} . $f_\textup{DM}$ was calculated via Equation \ref{fDM Calculation} for all subhaloes meeting this criteria, and subhaloes with $f_\textup{DM}<0.5$ were classified as DMDGs. Non-extreme DMDGS were DMDGS such that $f_\textup{DM}\geq0.05$ This reduced our resolved galaxy population to $15,595$ satellite galaxies for the literature reproduction. Of these $513$ were classified as DMDGs ($3.3 \%$). When excluding extreme DMDGS ($f_\textup{DM}\leq 0.05$), we are left with $400$ ($2.5\%$) non-extreme DMDGs. We notice that the original analysis of Illustris (the predecessor to TNG) from the literature includes only $1.5\%$. We acknowledge this in \ref{Literature Discussion}.

To establish sample subhaloes used for environmental and merger analysis, we use a $Z-$score algorithm to derive the $5$ subhalos, meeting required criteria, with the least average deviation across logarithmic $M_\star \odot$, logarithmic $M_{200},$ and $f_\textup{DM}$. We do not include extreme DMDGs in the selected catalo for this analysis. $Z-$score is calculated with Equation \ref{Z-score} for each measure, and the deviation (total distance from median) is calculated using Equation \ref{Standardized Distance}. We extract the five galaxies with the lowest reported deviation and report them during designated analysis. When analyzing merger history and environment, we use representative (low deviation) galaxies in order to get a representation without high computational weight. These five galaxies are not enough to create a full generalizable study of all DMDGs, but may provide us with relevant reference information and statistics.
\begin{equation}
\label{Z-score}
\box{\textup{Z-Score} = |\frac{\textup{Galaxy Value} - \textup{Median}}{\textup{Mean Absolute Deviation}}|}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{Standardized Distance}
\textup{Deviation} = \sqrt{Z_{\textup{Log Stellar Mass}}^2+Z_{\textup{Total Mass}}^2+Z_f_{\textup{DM}}^2}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Subhalo Behavior and Movement} \label{Subhalo Behavior and Movement}
To understand the evolutionary histories correlated with DMDGs, we interpret merger trees provided by the TNG 300-1 catalog. Our analysis is carried out independently for specified subhalo IDs at selected snapshots. A SubFindID is assigned by TNG 300-1 and connected to corresponding subhalos across different snapshots and merger events via the SubLink algorithm. Corresponding or shared particles are used to identify potential descendant candidates and ranked by particle binding-energy. To describe and identify the mergers experienced by a galaxy, we separate the merger branches and identify if any significant branch is found at the specific snapshot. 

To analyze whether subhalos are affected by their environment or behavior, we record distance from the central subhalo across a range of snapshots. The TNG 300-1 catalog allows us to find the corresponding group number for specified SubfindID and identify the ID of the corresponding central galaxy. Comparing the ID allows us to easily distinguish whether the analyzed subhalo is a central or satellite galaxy. For galaxies identified at satellites, we retrieve the position measurement (provided by TNG 300-1) and calculate $r=|\textup{x}_\textup{satellite}-\textup{x}_\textup{central}|$, where $\textup{x}$ are three-dimensional coordinates of the center of each subhalo ($(x,y,z)$), and account for periodic boundary correction along coordinates within the $300 \textup{ Mpc}$ box. To account for the scale of the subhalos and larger group halo, we normalize over $R_{200}$ (radius enclosing mean density $200$ times the universal critical density). The corresponding value of $\frac{r}{R_{200}}$ is calculated across multiple snapshots to find the change in normalized distance between the central and satellite over time. We graph these measurements against redshift to find the overall trend. Because analysis is carried out across multiple snapshots, and processes such as infall and mergers may disrupt the satellite/central status of a subhalo, we reevaluate $\textup{x}_\textup{satellite}$ and $\textup{x}_\textup{central}$ at each snapshot. \cite{10.1111/j.1365-2966.2005.09543.x} 


\subsection{Machine Learning Analysis Methodology} \label{Machine Learning Analysis Methodology}
To analyze predictive feature importance and accuracy of different halo metrics on $f_\textup{DM}$, we employ Random Forest (RF) Regression and Classifying algorithm for our given subhalo groups \cite{sciKitLearn}. We analyze accuracy using $R^2$ metrics, given by Equation \ref{R Squared}, where $y_i$ is the actual target value (of $f_\textup{DM}$), $\hat{y}_i$ is the predicted value, and $\overline{y}$ is the mean of the target values within the dataset. We use $3$ "control" galaxies for each non-extreme DMDG. Control galaxies are found using a scalar fit function \cite{scikit-learn} using logarithmic stellar mass $M_\star$ and logarithmic halo mass $M_{200}/M_\odot$. We employ a test set of $20\%$, a random seed of $42$, $500$ estimators with a minimum split sample of $2$, and a minimum leaf sample of $1$. 
\begin{equation}
\label{R Squared}
R^2=1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\overline{y}_i)^2}
\end{equation}

For RF classification, we use the same $1:3$ DMDG to control sample to classify between DMDG and non-DMDG. We employ the same tree parameters (test set of $20\%$, a random seed of $42$, $500$ estimators with a minimum split sample of $2$, and a minimum leaf sample of $1$) and a balanced class weight. To deepen our feature analysis, we employ SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) \cite{DBLP:journals/corr/LundbergL17} for the given features.

We employ the criteria listed in \ref{Galaxy Selection Pipeline} in addition to our standard resolved galaxy selection (stellar mass $10^9 < M_\star < 10^{10} M_\odot$, host halo mass $M_{200} > 10^{13} M_\odot$, and a classification as a satellite galaxy by the TNG 300-1 catalog). This provides us with a catalog of $15,595$ resolved satellite galaxies, $513$ total DMDGS. The distribution of $f_\textup{DM}$ is modeled in Figure \ref{fDM in Literature Resolved Galaxies}. The median $f_\textup{DM}$ for DMDGs is $0.380$, including extreme DMDGS. The distribution features a left skew and median above the middle of the range indicating high $f_\textup{DM}$ is more common in DMDGs. The median $f_\textup{DM}$ for all selected satellites  is $0.784$. Furthermore, the median $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ is significantly lower in DMDGs ($1.257$) than in the whole population ($2.966$),

The distribution from Figure \ref{fDM in Literature Resolved Galaxies} shows an abnormal spike in the lowest $f_\textup{DM}$ ($f_\textup{DM} < 0.01$). This trend is also consistent with results from Jing et al., \cite{Jing_2019}. To account for this, Jing filters their catalog group into extreme and non-extreme DMDGs, where $0.05 \leq f_\textup{DM} < 0.5$ for non-extreme DMDGs. We apply this cut for our DMDG catalog and re-calculate median values to be $0.420$ for non-extreme DMDGs and $0.785$.  We employ a $t-$test to quantify the differing distributions and find a result of $-95.9$ with $p-$value of $0.0$. Jing references tidal stripping and displacement of central galaxies during mergers as a likely cause for the distribution \cite{2018MNRAS.474..580P}. We attempt to further clarify this trend in Section \ref{Merger History and Environmental Analysis}.

\subsection{Machine Learning Results} \label{Machine Learning Results}

\subsection{Discussion} \label{Literature Discussion}
In Jing et al., the primary formation process associated with DMDGs is expected to be tidal stripping as a result of colliding halos decentralizing the central subhalo and displacing baryonic mass from dark matter. Tidal stripping is a motivating opportunity because it provides a direct pathway without altering $\Lambda \textup{CDM}$ cosmology. Tidal stripping occurs due to frequent mergers between dark matter halos, accounted for in the $\Lambda \textup{CDM}$, and is consistent with gravitational processes. Although it is not the only possible candidate for DMDGs, it allows us to explain the presence of such objects without significantly altering cosmological models. DMDGs on their own oppose the ratios of dark matter and baryonic mass from the $\Lambda \textup{CDM}$. However, as galaxies are relatively small celestial bodies, and DMDGs account for only a small percent of all subhalos within our simulation, it is likely that these results are offset by the vast amount of non-DMDG structures within the universe.

Our results are consistent with, but do not prove, tidal stripping as a possible explanation for DMDGs both in hydrodynamical simulations and what is observed from observational cosmology. Figure \ref{fDM against radius} demonstrates a general trend that satellite galaxies located closer to their centrals (lower $\frac{r}{R_{200c}}$) were measured to have lower average $f_\textup{DM}$. Such galaxies are consistent with tidally stripped galaxies that had only been recently removed from the center of their host halo. This correlation is further supported by our SHAP analysis, which indicates roughly $-0.2$ predictive value for high $\frac{r}{R_{200c}}$, indicating that higher distance from central decreased the likelihood of classification as a DMDG. 

Our individualized analysis of subhalo $738558$ displayed frequent mergers are redshifts surrounding extreme drops in $f_\textup{DM}$ despite the subhalo retaining its central status at such points. Colliding halos could still provide similar mechanics to tidal stripping even in the absence of displacement from the center of the subhalo. It is possible tha strong gravitational force from colliding galaxies can strip loosely bound dark matter while leaving tightly clustered baryonic and stellar mass. Figure \ref{M vs r literature sample} demonstrates such an example, with stellar mass being more abundant in the more dense and difficult to strip interior of subhalos. 

A notable result from the individual analysis is that Subhalo $738558$ only became classified as a satellite at snapshot $80$ ($z=0.26$), slightly before its final shift from non-DMDG to DMDG at snapshot $85$ ($z=0.18$). Because we were only able to perform a full case study on one subhalo, it is unclear whether this trend will remain true for all DMDG satellites or whether being a satellite directly influences its status as a DMDG. We perform further analysis on the possibility for DMDG centrals in Section \ref{placeholder}. However, it is noteworthy that displacement from central to satellite galaxy typically occurs after a significant merger between halos redefining the central subhalo as the central subhalo from the colliding halo. This mechanism also allows for tidal stripping as gravity pulls the former central away from the center of the newly formed halo. 

There are a few limitations and inconsistencies with our data. First is that subhalo $738558$ experiences very low degree mergers throughout its history and none at snapshot $66$ or $80$. Tidal stripping is driven by processes like mergers, so it is unclear why there appears to be a lack of major mergers. This could be attributed to branches from the main progenitor or additional mergers unaccounted for by our SubLink algorithim, but it does propose evidence against tidal stripping. We also acknowledge that our DMDG percentile ($2.5\%$) is substantially larger than that of Jing et al. ($1.5\%$) We employ all of the same subhalo criteria and attribute this difference primarily to differences within the simulation test suites. However, this difference is indicative that our results are not direct replicas of theirs and present altered statistics and data as a result of differing simulation size and conditions.

\subsection{Discussion}
As a brief overview of the central DMDG analysis, we find that the trend does not dispute but is not particularly indicative of tidal stripping as a mechanism for DMDG formation. Tidal stripping is highly motivated by mergers displacing central galaxies and their surrounding mass. Central galaxies themselves thus could not have been "tidally stripped" but may have experienced the effects of tidal stripping. The external dark matter mass, typically located outside of the inner radius, could still be drawn by gravitational forces from mergers. 

Our case study galaxy, $1604368$, experienced mergers within $3$ snapshot values of each significant drop in $f_\textup{DM}$. However, similar to Subhalo $738558$, these were low-intensity mergers and experienced low mass ratios. This may be related to the smooth loss of $f_\textup{DM}$ depicted in Figure \ref{1604368 fDM progresssion}, although it is not directly clear whether this correlation is causal. Due to the minor mass ratios, we have little reason to believe this correlation is significant at all. 

Nearest satellite distance showed low predictive importance in RF classification and regression, despite a related measure, $\frac{r}{R_{200c}}$ showing the highest predictive importance. Tidal stripping would likely be supported by nearby satellite galaxies providing gravitational force to loosely bound dark matter. However, this correlation does not appear in our dataset. 

Noticeably, the distribution of $f_\textup{DM}$ for centrals (Figure \ref{Central DMDGS}) exhibits significantly less skew than that of the satellites from Jing et al. (Figure \ref{fDM in Literature Resolved Galaxies}). The data is roughly balanced around the median $0.741$. Thus, it is unclear whether the DMDGs are formed via significant and contrasting processes or rather makeup the lower end of a normal distribution. The lack of predictive accuracy for DMDGs also decreases the statistical significance of feature importance values in our RF classifier and regression for centrals. Our case study analysis of DMDG is also relatively shallow and does not encompass all possible formation pathways. Further analysis into a larger population of centrals may be necessary to fully understand the evolutionary processes behind these formations.

\section{$R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ Comparisson and Residuals}
Throughout both the literature selected satellite and central analysis, we find that the reported median $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ is significantly lower for the selected DMDG group than general dataset and controls. We further examine this relationship by accounting for the statistical distribution and residuals at constant $M_\star$.

$R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ is highly correlated to our measurement of $f_\textup{DM}$, as it is the chosen use of aperture encompassing stellar and dark matter mass (Equation \ref{f_DM Calculation}). The metric is given for each subhalo by the TNG 300-1 catalog and describes the radius in which half of the halos stellar mass is enclosed.

The distribution of $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ for all satellites following Jing et al. \cite{Jing_2019}, DMDG satellites, all resolved centrals, and DMDG centrals is given by Figure \ref{R_half_star_distribution}. For the remainder of the analysis, we refer to resolved satellites by Jing et al. as satellites and resolved centrals as centrals. For both the satellite and central groups, the DMDG subgroup exhibits significantly lower $R_\{textup{half}, \star\}$ and a less expansive upper range. General centrals, in particular, experienced an outlier with significantly higher $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ than its counterpart population. It is not clear whether the larger range is due to evolutionary pathways confining the available range of $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ or a generally smaller sample set. To test this distribution, we perform a $t-$test between the respective DMDG and general groups. For central galaxies, the $t-$test yields a statistic of $-5.30$ witha $p-$value of $1.13 \times 10^{-7}$. The satellites yield a $t-$test statistic of $-39.9$ with a $p-$value of $0.0$. The higher $p-$value in the central group is likely due to the smaller sample size of DMDGs. The recorded median values of $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ for centrals and central DMDGS are $3.419$ and $2.101$, respectively. The median values satellites and satellite DMDGs are $2.966$ and $1.257$, respectively. 

The strong variance in medians and overall distribution of $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ are preliminary evidence that DMDGs may exhibit more concentrated mass than their non-DMDG counterparts. This would be a pattern consistent with strong, tightly-bound stellar mass in contrast to loosely spread dark matter. Further analysis is nescessary to define this correlation and find if it is a distinct characteristic of DMDGs, correlated with the defining metrics of $f_\textup{DM}$.

In attempt to define a direct correlation between $f_\textup{DM}$ and $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$, we analyze the relationship in Figure \ref{R_half_star vs f_DM} for both satellites and centrals. Both show primarily positive correlations ($\rho=0.745$ and $\rho = 0.504$, respectively). This is consistent with the distribution from Figure \ref{R_half_star_distribution} such that smaller values $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$  were associated with DMDG status and thus lower $f_\textup{DM}$. The correlation is not inherently linear and includes a degree of scatter associated with stochatic halo formation processes. Although both satellites and centrals display a curved relationship, that of central galaxies appears to have a vertical spike and spread at the lowest level of $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$. 

Based on the relationship between $f_\textup{DM}$, $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ and $M_\star$, we form a predictive model using a second degree polynomial regression predicting logarithmic $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ based on an independent logarithmic stellar mass. We found that, for the centrals dataset excluding DMDGs, the second degree polynomial regression produces a higher $R^2$ predictive measurement than first and third degrees. We do not perform residual analysis on our satellite population due to a preliminary flat correlation between the two variables. The central polynomial regression equation is given by Equation \ref{expected residuals}.

\begin{equation}
\label{expected residuals}
\log R_{\textup{half,} \star} = 66.8553 -5.7237 \log M_\star +0.1246 \log M_\star^2
\end{equation}

We note that our regression is made solely off of non-DMDG centrals. This allows us to ensure the model is not over-fitted to DMDGs and that our residuals are reflective of comparisson to the control dataset. Residuals ar calculated both for DMDG and non-DMDG centrals. The residual is calculated by Equation \ref{residual calculation}. We model residuals against $M_\star$ in Figure \ref{second degree residuals}. Although the general residual trend is constant and centered around $0.0$, there is a diverging trend for DMDGs (indicated in red). The left pannel isolates this trend and displays a negative correlation. We employ a Mann-Whitney U statistical test, which produces a result of $285800$ with $p-$value $9.426 \times 10^{-57}$. The median residual for the control (non-DMDG) centrals was $-0.018$, and the median residual for DMDGs was $-0.582$. 

The key relationship shows that, across the range of $M_\star$, DMDGs show distinctly smaller values of $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ and hold a more compacted shape. Furthermore, more massive DMDGs have even more compact structure. This correlation may not be causal. $f_\textup{DM}$, $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ and $M_\star$ are inherently related through the calculation of $f_\textup{DM}$. However, this relationship alone is not enough to define the correlation between DMDGs. It is likely that the $R_{\textup{half}, \star}$ trend with $f_\textup{DM}$ may be a result of external stellar and dark matter mass being stripped through interactions and gravitational effects. More massive DMDGs may be subject to stronger gravitational effects or more strongly clustered stellar mass. Further analysis into the active formation of DMDGs is nescessary to designate the causal pathway for this relationship.

\end{equation}
